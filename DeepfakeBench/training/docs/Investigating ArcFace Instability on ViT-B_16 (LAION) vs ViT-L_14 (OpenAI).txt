 

Investigating ArcFace Instability on ViT-B/16 (LAION) vs ViT-L/14 (OpenAI)
Background and Observations
We observed that using ArcFace loss (additive angular margin) dramatically improved performance on a CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone, but caused training instability on a CLIP ViT-B/16 (LAION DataComp) backbone. In particular, for a binary real vs fake image detection task (with human face data):

• ViT-L/14 (OpenAI) + ArcFace: Achieved AUC ˜ 0.99, with stable training and clear separation between classes.
• ViT-B/16 (LAION) + ArcFace: Peaked around AUC 0.69 then declined to ~0.62, indicating divergence or oscillation in training. In contrast, ViT-B/16 with standard cross-entropy stayed stable around AUC 0.80+.
This discrepancy is puzzling, since both models are CLIP-based vision transformers. We aim to understand why ArcFace works on the larger L/14 model but fails on the B/16 model , and refine our approach accordingly.

Key Differences Between ViT-L/14 and ViT-B/16 (LAION)
Before analyzing ArcFace specifically, it's important to note the fundamental differences in the two backbones:

• Model Capacity: ViT-L/14 has 24 layers and a wider hidden size (˜1024), vs ViT-B/16 with 12 layers and hidden size 768. The large model naturally outperforms the base model on vision tasks	. In our context, the ViT-L/14 backbone showed significantly better generalization in detection tasks than ViT-B/16	.
• Embedding Dimension: The OpenAI L/14 model produces a ~1024-D image embedding (no additional projection needed), whereas the LAION B/16 model outputs a 512-D embedding (projected from 768). A smaller embedding might provide less room for angular separation.
• Pretraining Data: OpenAI's CLIP L/14 was trained on 400M WebImageText pairs (likely rich in diverse content including human faces). The OpenCLIP B/16 was trained on the LAION DataComp-1B subset (filtered from LAION-5B). Differences in these datasets could lead to different feature distributions. For example, OpenAI CLIP models were trained on a private WIT dataset, while LAION models trained on filtered web data with potentially different biases	.
• Attention Implementation: The OpenAI model uses separate Q, K, V projection layers, while the
OpenCLIP model uses a fused	weight for QKV. Our SVD-based adaptation initially missed
the QKV for B/16 (limiting trainable parameters), though we later fixed this. After the fix, B/16 had
~73k trainable params (vs ~198k in L/14) under the Effort method.
• Normalized Features: Both models use CLIP's training objective which involves normalizing embeddings for contrastive loss. ArcFace operates on L2-normalized features and weights,

effectively placing features on a unit hypersphere	. Any difference in how the backbones handle feature norms or the final LayerNorm could impact training when using ArcFace.
In summary, ViT-B/16 is a smaller, lower-dimensional model , and its pretraining/architecture details differ from the ViT-L/14. These differences set the stage for why ArcFace might behave differently.

Why ArcFace May Be Unstable on B/16 (Analysis of Hypotheses)

ArcFace loss introduces an angular margin	between a feature and its correct class center, and a scaling
factor	to amplify logits. This is known to greatly enhance class separability, especially for face
recognition tasks	. However, ArcFace (and similar margin losses) must be used with care - margin and scale hyperparameters play a crucial role in training stability	. We examine several possible reasons why ArcFace destabilized the B/16 model:

• 1. Margin and Scale Too High for a Smaller Model:
Larger margins make the classification task stricter - the model must separate classes by a fixed angular gap. If the margin is too large relative to the model's capacity or the feature dimensionality, training can become unstable	. In our case, we used margin $m=0.28$ (˜16°) and final scale
$s˜18$ for both models. This worked for L/14, but B/16 might require a gentler setting. Research on ArcFace notes that "an excessively large [margin] may destabilize training"	, especially on smaller or imbalanced datasets. The 512-D embedding of B/16 might not accommodate a 16° margin as easily as L/14's ~1024-D space. In practice, margin-based losses often need to be tuned lower for lower- dimensional embeddings or smaller networks. Our experiments already hinted that lowering $s$ (12-15) didn't solve the issue by itself, so the margin $m$ might need to be reduced (or introduced more gradually) for B/16.

• 2. Broad Intra-Class Variance (Multi-modal Classes):
Unlike face recognition where each class (person) is relatively compact in feature space, here the binary classes "real" vs "fake" encompass high diversity. The "fake" class in particular may include multiple generation methods and visual styles, effectively forming multiple sub-clusters. ArcFace assumes each class can be represented by a single weight vector (class center) with samples tightly clustered around it	. If a class is multi-modal, forcing a single angular margin for all its samples can be problematic. Some fake samples might lie close to the real class direction or far from the fake class center; imposing the margin on all will yield conflicting gradients. Essentially, ArcFace provides a "local" angular constraint (feature vs its class center)  but no guarantee of optimal global separation if the class itself isn't cohesive	. This could lead to the model oscillating as it tries to swing the class center to accommodate different subsets of fake images. The larger L/14 model, with more representational power, might implicitly cluster the "fake" variations better (or had learned more universal features for faces, given its stronger performance). The smaller B/16 might lack this flexibility, so the margin magnifies the issue. In literature, solutions like sub-center ArcFace allow multiple centers per class to address multi-modal data and stabilize training	, but we did not use such a mechanism here.

• 3. Feature Norm and Gradient Dynamics:
ArcFace normalizes features and weights, meaning the loss depends purely on angles. However, during training the gradient through the normalization operation can be sensitive to feature norm distributions. If the B/16 model outputs vary more in norm (since the final embedding is a

learned projection to 512D), the backpropagated gradients might be uneven. The L/14 model (which outputs 1024D directly from transformer + LN) might produce more stable unit-length features suitable for ArcFace. We suspect B/16's features may have required additional normalization or a smaller learning rate. Notably, ArcFace effectively amplifies gradient magnitudes by scaling logits with $s$ (we used up to 18). A smaller model might not cope with these larger gradient updates without a lower base learning rate. The fact that B/16 + ArcFace began to diverge after the warmup (when $s$ reached its max) suggests gradients became too large for stable convergence. In contrast, the no-ArcFace run (standard softmax) had much smoother gradients at the same learning rate.

• 4. Pretraining Representation Differences:
OpenAI's CLIP L/14 and LAION's CLIP B/16, despite similar objectives, could encode images differently. It's possible that L/14's embedding space is more linearly separable for real vs fake out-of-the-box (perhaps picking up on subtle artifacts in faces), whereas B/16's embedding might align less with the real/fake distinction initially. If L/14 already separates the classes to some extent, ArcFace fine-tuning can quickly exploit that and add a margin. If B/16's initial separation is poor, ArcFace might struggle, as the model must first discover the class separation and enforce the margin simultaneously with limited trainable parameters. The Effort method freezes most pre-trained weights, so any inherent advantage in the pretrained features matters a lot. The superior generalization of CLIP-L/14 over CLIP-B/16 on various tasks is well-documented	. We intend to verify this by testing OpenAI's ViT-B/16 model under ArcFace - if it trains more stably than the LAION B/16, it would implicate the pretraining differences (data or training recipe) rather than just model size.

• 5. Limited Adaptation Capacity:
Under the Effort approach, we only fine-tune ~0.04-0.07% of parameters (via low-rank SVD adapters). For the large model, ~198k parameters spread across 96 attention layers might have been sufficient to learn the margin-based separation. For the base model, ~74k parameters across 48 layers might be too few degrees of freedom to both capture forgery cues and satisfy the margin constraint. In other words, the B/16 might need a bit more flexibility (e.g. rank-2 adapters or tuning the final projection layer)  to handle ArcFace, otherwise it gets stuck in a suboptimal regime. This is a more speculative point, but it aligns with the intuition that smaller models sometimes need proportionally more fine-tuning to achieve the same effect.

Refining the Plan (Next Steps)
Given the above analysis, we are adjusting our approach to diagnose and address the instability:

• A. Isolate Architecture vs Data: We will run the Effort+ArcFace training on OpenAI's ViT-B/16 CLIP model. If this model (which shares architecture with LAION B/16 but was trained on OpenAI's data) converges successfully with ArcFace , it implies the issue was likely related to the LAION model's learned feature space. If it also fails, the culprit is more about architecture/capacity and loss settings.

• B. Tune ArcFace Hyperparameters:	We will experiment with a	smaller angular margin  (e.g.
$m=0.15$) for the B/16 model. Literature indicates that choosing an appropriate margin is crucial -
too large margin can destabilize training	. We'll also try reducing the end scaling factor (e.g.
$s_{end}=12$ or 14 instead of 18) and/or gradually annealing the margin from 0 to target over the

first few epochs (similar to how we annealed $s$). The goal is to ease the model into the high separation regime.

• C. Lower Learning Rate: Since ArcFace effectively increases the difficulty of the task, a lower base LR might prevent divergence. We plan to test LR in the range $5\times10^{-5}$ to $1\times10^{-4}$ (with longer warmup) for B/16 + ArcFace. A smaller learning rate could accommodate the sharper loss landscape induced by the margin	.

• D. Feature Space Analysis: We will deepen our research by extracting features from both models on a sample of real and fake images	without fine-tuning . By comparing their cosine similarity distributions and norm statistics, we can see if B/16's features are inherently less separable or more varied than L/14's. For example, we'll compute the mean intra-class vs inter-class cosine similarity for each model. This might reveal that L/14 starts with a better angle between real and fake clusters than B/16, explaining the ease of ArcFace training.

• E. Investigate Multi-Modal Fake Handling: If hypothesis (2) holds true (fake class being multi- modal), one workaround could be to explicitly account for different fake types. In the interim, we might try training with per-fake-type classification (if labels for different fake techniques are available) or using a Sub-Center ArcFace approach (where the "fake" class is allowed multiple centers)	. This is an advanced idea and may be beyond our initial scope, but it's a potential future direction if simply tuning hyperparameters doesn't yield stability.

• F. Monitor Gradients and Norms: We will add logging to monitor the	feature norms, weight norms, and gradient norms during training. If we see, for instance, B/16's feature norms oscillating or gradient spikes coinciding with ArcFace margin enforcement, it will give concrete evidence of what's going wrong internally (e.g., are certain layers saturating or is the classification weight vector oscillating?).

By conducting the above steps, we aim to pinpoint the root cause of ArcFace's incompatibility with the ViT-B/ 16 LAION backbone and find a remedy. Our expectation is that	scaling down the difficulty of ArcFace (margin/scale) and/or improving B/16's effective capacity will stabilize training. ArcFace has shown great success on the L/14 model (and is historically proven on face recognition tasks), so we are confident the concept is sound - we likely just need to adapt it properly to the smaller model.

Conclusion
The preliminary conclusion is that the ArcFace loss introduced a tougher optimization challenge than the ViT-B/16 (LAION) model could handle under our initial settings . The ViT-L/14's higher capacity and perhaps more face-relevant feature space allowed ArcFace to shine (consistent with ArcFace's intended use on high-quality face features). The B/16, on the other hand, struggled due to a combination of lower dimensional embeddings, possibly less separable initial features, and the strict angular margin exacerbating its limitations. This resulted in training instability manifesting as peaking and then declining performance - a clear sign of divergence.

Moving forward, we will leverage the above research insights to adjust our training strategy. By doing so, we aim to either get ArcFace to work with ViT-B/16 (making it a faster lightweight alternative to L/14) or

conclude that this backbone is fundamentally unsuited for margin-based fine-tuning in our scenario. Either outcome will be valuable for guiding our project's next steps.
